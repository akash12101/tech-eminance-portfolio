When to Hire a Developer vs When to Outsource

TE Tech Eminence Team · Jun 2026 · 6 min read
In This Article

  • The Real Cost of Outsourcing
  • Five Questions That Lead to the Right Decision

At some point, every growing business or agency hits the same wall. A good brief lands. The budget is right. The client is ready to move. And then someone says it: "We don't have the dev capacity for this."

What happens next shapes the next two or three years of your business. Some teams hire. Some find a development partner and quietly deliver the project anyway.

This guide is for business and agency owners who want a real framework for making that call — not a vague pros-and-cons list. A set of specific questions that lead to a concrete answer.

The Real Cost of Hiring a Developer

Let's start with the number most owners underestimate.

A developer on a $65,000 salary does not cost you $65,000. Add employer payroll taxes, benefits, equipment, software licenses, recruiting fees, and the management time it takes to onboard someone properly. You're looking at closer to $85,000 in the first year. Often more.

And that assumes the hire goes smoothly. If you use a recruiter, add 15 to 20 percent of the first-year salary on top of that.

There's also the ramp-up period to factor in. Most developers take three to six months to reach full productivity in a new environment. You're paying full cost before you're getting full output.

The quiet months problem. Client work ebbs and flows. Your developer's salary does not. During slow periods, that fixed cost sits on your books whether or not there's work to fill their time.

None of this means hiring is wrong. It means you need to be honest about what you're actually signing up for when you do it.

The Real Cost of Outsourcing

Outsourcing is not free margin either.

You share the profit. A project you invoice at $8,000 might cost $4,500 to deliver through a development partner. That leaves $3,500. With an in-house developer, the margin is higher on paper — but only if that developer is fully utilized across paid projects, which most are not.

You also give up some direct control. The build happens at a distance. Communication is routed through your partner's team structure rather than going directly to the developer. Response times depend on your partner's process.

These are genuine trade-offs, not reasons to avoid the model. Just things worth factoring in honestly before you start.

Five Questions That Lead to the Right Decision

Rather than a generic comparison, here are five questions that narrow the decision down quickly.

1. Is this a one-off brief or a repeating service line?

If a single client needs a Shopify store built and you have no other plans in that area, hiring a Shopify developer is a big commitment to one project. A development partner is a far lower-risk way to deliver it.

If you're winning three Shopify briefs a month and that number is growing, the math changes. Consistent volume is the foundation of a hire that makes financial sense.

2. Do you have 12 months of pipeline to justify the salary?

This is the most important question on the list. A developer needs consistent billable work to justify their cost. If you cannot honestly forecast enough hours to cover their salary for at least a year, you're taking on a liability.

Be honest with yourself about the difference between expected pipeline and confirmed pipeline.

3. How specialised is the skill?

Some skills are narrow. Headless commerce builds, AI chatbot integrations, and complex Laravel applications are not roles you can fill with a generalist hire. You need specific expertise, and finding it takes time.

A development partner who specializes in these areas already has the team, the tooling, and the project history. You're not building that from zero.

4. How quickly do you need to start delivering?

A hire takes time — job post, interviews, offer, notice period, start date. Three months minimum in most markets. Often five or six months for experienced developers.

A development partner with a proper onboarding process can be briefed and delivering within days. If a client is waiting, that difference matters.

5. What happens if your biggest client leaves?

Almost every business has one client who accounts for a disproportionate chunk of revenue. If that client churned tomorrow, could you still justify the developer's monthly cost?

This is not a reason to never hire. It's a reason to understand the risk you're taking on when you do.

When Hiring Makes More Sense

There are situations where hiring is clearly the right call.

  • You have 12 months or more of confirmed, billable pipeline in that skill area across multiple clients
  • The work is so central to your business that clients expect direct access to the person building it
  • You have the financial runway to carry the cost through slower months without stress
  • You're making a deliberate long-term decision to build an internal team as a competitive advantage

If all of those are true, hire. Build the team. Own the capability fully.

When Outsourcing Makes More Sense

Most businesses reading this are not in that position yet. And that's not a criticism — it just means the math points elsewhere.

Outsourcing works well when you're testing whether a new service line has legs before committing to headcount. It works when you win specialist briefs that fall outside your core team's skill set. When you need overflow capacity when your existing developers are fully utilised. When you need to start delivering fast.

It also works when you want to protect margins without adding fixed cost to your structure. A development partner scales with your revenue. A salary does not.

The Hybrid Approach Most Teams Land On

In practice, most established businesses and agencies use both.

One or two in-house developers handle the day-to-day work, client-facing conversations, and project management. A development partner handles specialist builds, overflow volume, and service lines the business hasn't built internal capacity for.

The business owns the relationship. The partner owns the build. The client sees a single, cohesive team.

This is not a compromise. For many businesses, it's the most profitable and scalable structure available. You get the speed and specialisation of a larger team without the fixed cost of employing one. If you want to understand how that model works in practice, our Web Design and Development page covers the full partnership structure, including how briefing, communication, and delivery actually work day to day.

A Note on Confidentiality

The most common objection to white-label outsourcing is that clients will find out.

In a well-run partnership, they don't. A proper white-label agreement puts a mutual NDA in place before any work begins. All work is delivered under your brand. There's no direct contact between your partner and your client.

We've worked this way with agency and business partners since 2011. In that time, client confidentiality has never been an issue. The model only works if it's invisible, and that's exactly what a properly structured partnership ensures.
